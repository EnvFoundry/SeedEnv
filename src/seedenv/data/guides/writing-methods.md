# Composition methods

Read only the section that fits the work. These are options for organizing meaning,
not a required outline or a requirement to create planning files.

## Scientific paper

Identify the paper's purpose and contribution. Original research may offer an
explanation, theorem, construction, measurement, method or resolved disagreement;
compare it with the closest prior result under the relevant conditions. A replication
tests an earlier result, a review synthesizes evidence, and a negative finding can
rule out an expected effect or route. Describe that contribution on its own terms.
Keep claims within the evidence without silently reducing the research goal.

Organize results by the questions they settle. Explain why a derivation or experiment
was needed before giving its outcome. Put comparisons on common quantities and
conditions; distinguish a new capability from a new implementation of a known one.
Move setup detail out of the main argument only when the reader can still follow it.

Build the abstract around the problem, what was done, the actual result and why it
changes understanding. This is a logic, not a fixed four-sentence template. State
concrete quantities when they express the result; do not fill it with claims about
rigor, comprehensiveness or transformative potential. Choose a title that identifies
the subject and finding without promising more than the work establishes.

For theoretical work, expose the decisive construction or implication. Do not bury
the new idea among preliminary lemmas. For experiments, make the comparison and
measurement understandable. For methods, show what becomes possible and what the
nearest alternative could already do. Keep negative or conflicting results when
they change the proposed explanation.

## Book or sustained explanation

Choose the reader's starting point and the understanding they should gain. Order
chapters by conceptual dependencies or a deliberate narrative progression, not by
the dates files were written. Introduce a term once, reuse it consistently, and
explain a changed meaning. A chapter should leave the reader able to understand
something the previous chapter did not establish.

Use a worked case to carry difficult ideas, then vary the case to show what
generalizes. Keep essential examples, proofs and counterexamples even if they take
space. Remove recaps that repeat rather than connect. Maintain one existing outline
and terminology reference when the manuscript is too long to hold at once; retrieve
the relevant earlier passages instead of reconstructing them from memory.

## Report or decision document

State the decision and the comparison that governs it. Separate established
conditions from assumptions. Explain how the options differ on the factors that
would change the choice. A practical recommendation needs a reason and an applicable
setting, not a universal ranking. Keep operational detail where the person carrying
out the recommendation can find it.

## Revision and bilingual adaptation

Revise in the order that prevents wasted rewriting: substance and structure first,
then paragraphs and sentences, then formatting. This is not a requirement for three
separate review passes. Fix a local problem locally when the surrounding argument
already works.

Translate the relationships and intended register, not the source language's word
order. Preserve quantities, units, technical terms and strength of claims. Use a
terminology note only when recurring terms need coordination across a long work.
Do not attach both language versions unless requested or useful to the deliverable.
